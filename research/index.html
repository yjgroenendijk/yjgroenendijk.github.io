    Research

    Human-centred technology and empathic design

    I study digital transformation as an ongoing social and economic transition. My research asks how technologies mediate interactions between people and their environments across everyday life, work, education and public services, with particular attention to ageing, care and community settings.

    Empathic design of technology brings people’s perspectives and everyday practices into design and evaluation. In my Living Lab work, that means examining how a technology fits residents’ routines, what it asks of care staff, and how neighbourhoods and institutions shape who can use it. Studying use, adaptation and non-use helps identify what makes a technology meaningful, responsible and responsive to different needs.

    Research cases

    Memory as Care · Who gets to tell Rotterdam’s story?

    The lives of older Chinese residents are part of Rotterdam’s history, yet their experiences are rarely visible in the city’s public memory. This participatory oral-history project asks how residents can share memories on their own terms and how those memories might contribute to recognition and belonging.

    As a co-applicant, I helped shape the proposal and contribute to the oral-history design. The project brings together Erasmus University Rotterdam, Erasmus MC, TU/e, Verhalenhuis Belvédère and Stichting Perspectief Chinezen in Nederland. The planned work moves from community memory cafés to voluntary follow-up interviews that use photographs to prompt recollection. A public exhibition is planned as a separate step, which raises its own choices about what can be shared and how.

    Ageing in place · What makes a neighbourhood inhabitable, inclusive, and accessible in everyday life?

    At TU/e, I work on ELLI and MOVUS, projects that bring questions of ageing and care into the design of homes and neighbourhoods. In ELLI, the Living Labs in Waalre and Emmen bring residents and local partners into the development and testing of interventions.

    My contribution is to turn broad concepts such as accessibility, participation and agency into questions and observations that a multidisciplinary team can use in the field. A route can be physically accessible yet still be difficult to use if it is unfamiliar, lacks a place to rest or depends on someone being available to accompany a resident. This distinction helps frame what we look for in the field. It also informs my research on how smart technologies fit care routines and what shapes their use or non-use.

    Migratory Birds in the Digital Age · How do people make a home across places?

    My doctoral research followed older Chinese seasonal migrants known as Houniao. I combined interviews, observation and digital ethnography to examine how they organised everyday life across two homes and how they narrated their mobility online.

    In one study, I analysed 121 Douyin vlogs from two migration periods. The videos showed older creators using local expressions to build relationships with viewers, documenting routines associated with wellbeing, and presenting later life as a time of continued exploration. The study traces how physical journeys and online storytelling become part of the same process of making a place feel like home.

    How I work

    Mediated nearby

    I use the idea of the mediated nearby to ask how the people, places and routines that matter become reachable in everyday life. This connects my earlier work on mobility and digital place-making with my current research on care, neighbourhoods and belonging.

    Choosing a method for the question

    An interview can show how someone makes sense of a transition. Observation can reveal where everyday routines depend on other people or on the design of a space. Digital ethnography can show how an account changes when people tell it to an audience. I choose and combine methods according to what each can make visible, and I use narrative, thematic or framing analysis according to the question and materials.

    Making research ethics part of the design

    I have prepared ethics applications and data management plans for my own research. In participatory oral history, a design question is how to separate agreeing to an interview from agreeing to the public use of a named quotation or a family photograph. These choices affect the consent process, what the team records and retains, and what can appear in an exhibition.
