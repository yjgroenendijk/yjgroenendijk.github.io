    Research

    Social relationships, institutions and change

    My research examines how people navigate social relationships, institutions and technological change, with a particular focus on ageing, migration, care and participation. I am interested in how people maintain agency, build social connections and create a sense of belonging within changing social and institutional environments.

    Drawing on ethnography, narrative inquiry and participatory approaches, I connect everyday experiences with broader questions of inequality, accessibility and collective responsibility. I study these processes across digital environments, organisations and local communities.

    Research trajectory

    My early work examined social justice, educational policy and institutional practices. My doctoral research moved to ageing, migration and digitally mediated belonging. My current research extends these questions to care environments, organisational practices, participation and the social conditions that shape access to support.

    Research cases

    Migratory Birds in the Digital Age · How do people make a home across places?

    My doctoral research followed older Chinese seasonal migrants known as Houniao. I combined interviews, observation and digital ethnography to examine how they organised everyday life across two homes, connected with others and narrated their mobility online.

    In one study, I analysed 121 Douyin vlogs from two migration periods. The videos showed older creators using local expressions to build relationships with viewers, documenting routines associated with wellbeing and presenting later life as a time of continued exploration. This research traces how physical journeys and online storytelling contribute to a sense of home and social belonging.

    Ageing in place · What makes a neighbourhood inclusive and accessible in everyday life?

    At TU/e, I work on ELLI and MOVUS, projects that bring questions of ageing and care into the design of homes and neighbourhoods. In ELLI, Living Labs in Waalre and Emmen bring residents and local partners into the development and testing of interventions.

    My contribution includes translating concepts such as accessibility, participation, agency and person–environment fit into field questions, observations and research instruments that multidisciplinary teams can use. A route can be physically accessible yet difficult to use if it is unfamiliar, lacks a place to rest or depends on support that is not available. I also examine how smart technologies fit care routines and what shapes their use, adaptation or non-use.

    Memory as Care · Who gets to tell Rotterdam’s story?

    The lives of older Chinese residents are part of Rotterdam’s history, yet their experiences are rarely visible in the city’s public memory. This funded participatory oral-history project examines how residents can share memories on their own terms and how those memories may contribute to recognition and belonging.

    As a co-applicant, I helped shape the proposal and contribute to the oral-history design. The project brings together Erasmus University Rotterdam, Erasmus MC, TU/e, Verhalenhuis Belvédère and Stichting Perspectief Chinezen in Nederland. Planned activities include community memory cafés, voluntary oral-history interviews supported by photo-elicitation, and a public exhibition with separate decisions about consent and public use.

    Emerging research agenda · Shared responsibility and collective capabilities

    Building on my research on ageing, migration and care environments, I am interested in how social networks, care organisations, local authorities and communities develop the collective capabilities needed to support inclusive ageing. I want to examine how care responsibilities are negotiated, how technologies reshape relationships of support, and how people’s participation can inform more equitable and sustainable services.

    Potential empirical settings include dementia-friendly neighbourhoods, long-term care and community-based support. This is a developing research agenda that builds on my current projects, rather than a claim that these governance questions have already been resolved.

    How I work

    Mediated nearby

    I use the idea of the mediated nearby to ask how the people, places and routines that matter become reachable in everyday life. This connects my earlier work on mobility and digital place-making with my current research on care, neighbourhoods and belonging.

    Choosing a method for the question

    An interview can show how someone makes sense of a transition. Observation can reveal where everyday routines depend on other people or on the design of a space. Digital ethnography can show how an account changes when people tell it to an audience. I choose and combine methods according to what each makes visible, using narrative, thematic or framing analysis as appropriate to the question and material.

    Making research ethics part of the design

    I have prepared ethics applications and data management plans for my own research. In participatory oral history, one design question is how to separate agreement to an interview from agreement to the public use of a named quotation or family photograph. These choices affect consent, data stewardship and the material that can be presented in an exhibition.
